The Last Light Of Earth

A psychological horror epic of extinction, mutation, betrayal, and the end of humanity.

Featuring Foulnipple and KB, the twin architects of annihilation, and Fichael, the last man foolish enough to believe the world can be saved.

Prologue: The Day the Sun Turned Red

The end of the world did not begin with a nuclear explosion. It began with a sound. At 3:17 a.m. on October 31, 2031, every telephone on Earth rang simultaneously. Phones buried beneath rubble rang. Phones at the bottom of the ocean rang. Phones in abandoned hospitals rang from beneath piles of bodies. Even disconnected telephones in forgotten houses rang, their plastic shells trembling against dusty tables. Those who answered heard their own voices. Not recordings of conversations they remembered. Not echoes of words they had once spoken. They heard themselves screaming from the future. At 3:19 a.m., the first stars disappeared. At 3:21 a.m., the moon cracked down the middle. And at 3:24 a.m., something beneath the Earth's crust opened its eyes. The first mutations appeared in the cities. People collapsed in the streets, their spines bending backward as their bones softened like wax. Their mouths split wider than their skulls could support. Some died instantly. Others rose again minutes later, stumbling through the streets with milky eyes and an insatiable hunger for living flesh. The military called them Class-One Infected. The survivors called them zombies. Within seventy-two hours, the distinction no longer mattered. The dead were only the beginning. In the forests, deer grew additional limbs and hunted in packs. Dogs developed translucent skin, revealing organs that glowed with an unnatural blue light. Things that had once been human crawled across ceilings on fingers that had multiplied into clusters of twitching black tendrils. The oceans began boiling in isolated patches. Crops turned gray. Rain fell black in some countries and crimson in others. Then came the red aurora. It spread across the sky like a wound that refused to close. Scientists watched their instruments record impossible readings: gravity fluctuating, electromagnetic fields reversing, and energy signatures emanating from the two most densely infected locations on Earth. Two figures had emerged from the catastrophe. Two boys who had become something that humanity had never possessed the language to describe. Their names were Foulnipple and KB. And while the world was busy dying, they were learning how to make it suffer.

Part I: The Kings Beneath the Ashes

Nobody knew where Foulnipple had come from. Some survivors claimed he had been a normal human before the Red Aurora, a quiet boy who had discovered something buried beneath an abandoned research facility. Others insisted he had never been human at all. According to one rumor, his body had died during the first outbreak, but his consciousness had survived inside the electromagnetic field that surrounded the planet. The truth was worse. Foulnipple had discovered that reality itself possessed a nervous system. Every living thing, every atom, every distant star was connected through a structure of energy that scientists had never detected. When the world began mutating, Foulnipple could feel that structure. He could hear its vibrations. He could reach into it and pull. At first, he could bend a spoon without touching it. Then he crushed a military helicopter into a sphere of twisted metal while it was still flying. By the seventh day, he could extinguish the electrical grid of an entire continent with a thought. His eyes became incandescent red furnaces. When he focused on something, concentrated beams of energy erupted from his pupils, carving molten trenches through buildings and turning armored vehicles into puddles of glowing metal. He could see the heat signatures of living organisms through kilometers of concrete. He could hear the electrical impulses of a human brain and, with sufficient concentration, interrupt the signals that controlled the heart. But the laser eyes were merely the most visible expression of his power. Foulnipple could manipulate gravity, bending streets upward like ribbons and suspending entire buildings in the air. He could create localized singularities that consumed everything nearby. He could tear open microscopic holes in space and use them to transport himself across continents. And when he became angry, the laws of physics began to negotiate with him. He could make fire freeze. He could make sound arrive before the event that produced it. He could stand beneath a collapsing skyscraper and decide that, for him, gravity no longer applied. Yet the most terrifying thing about Foulnipple was not his strength. It was his patience. He did not kill every human he encountered. He sometimes left survivors alive for weeks, watching them struggle to preserve tiny pockets of civilization. He allowed them to build shelters, plant crops, and convince themselves that tomorrow might be better. Then he would appear in their skies. He would raise one hand. And their carefully reconstructed world would disappear. Foulnipple did not want humanity dead because he hated humanity. He wanted humanity to understand that it had never been in control. KB, however, had a different philosophy. If Foulnipple was the hand that crushed the world, KB was the mind that taught the world to scream. His appearance had changed in a different way. His hair hung in uneven, dark strands across his forehead, and the skin around his glowing red eyes was fractured with blackened fissures. The cracks extended across his cheeks and down his neck, like a porcelain mask breaking from the inside. Behind his eyes existed an intelligence that no longer functioned like a human mind. KB had evolved beyond ordinary perception. He could observe probabilities, examine branching timelines, and sense the emotional states of living beings from vast distances. He could enter dreams, implant false memories, and make entire groups of people believe that their closest friends had betrayed them. His telepathy could stretch across continents. His illusions were so precise that the brain accepted them as reality. Victims could feel wounds that did not exist, hear their children begging for help when those children were already dead, or spend what felt like decades trapped inside a memory that lasted only seconds. He could rearrange matter at the molecular level, creating biological weapons from the remains of the infected. He could regenerate from almost any physical injury, provided even a fragment of his consciousness survived. He could absorb the mutations of the creatures around him and improve upon them. And he could steal the strongest emotions of a person and turn them into weapons. Fear became a physical force. Grief became a gravitational anchor. Rage became a contagious biological mutation. Foulnipple and KB discovered one another on the ninth day of the apocalypse, standing on opposite ends of a ruined suspension bridge outside Chicago. Neither attacked. They simply looked at each other. Foulnipple saw a mind capable of understanding eternity. KB saw a being capable of bending eternity into whatever shape he desired. They understood, without speaking, that together they could accomplish something neither could achieve alone. Foulnipple would control the universe. KB would control everything living inside it. They shook hands. Every infected organism within fifty miles fell silent. Then, simultaneously, they looked toward the surviving cities. Foulnipple smiled. KB began to laugh. And beneath the ruins of civilization, millions of zombies turned toward the same direction. Their masters had arrived.
